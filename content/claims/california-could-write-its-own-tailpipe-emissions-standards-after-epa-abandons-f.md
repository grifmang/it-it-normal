summary: >-
  California has long-standing authority under the Clean Air Act to set its own
  vehicle emissions standards when granted a waiver by EPA, but this authority
  exists independent of EPA federal policy changes. Recent EPA actions have
  weakened federal tailpipe emissions standards, but California's ability to set
  stricter standards depends on maintaining its Clean Air Act waiver, which the
  Trump administration has previously attempted to revoke.

evidenceFor:
  - >-
    Section 209(b) of the Clean Air Act grants California unique authority to
    adopt vehicle emissions standards stricter than federal standards when EPA
    grants a waiver
  - >-
    California has historically received dozens of waivers from EPA to implement
    its own vehicle emissions standards since the 1970s
  - >-
    Other states can adopt California's standards but cannot create their own
    separate vehicle emissions rules
  - >-
    EPA announced in 2025 that it would weaken federal tailpipe emissions
    standards for model years 2027-2030
evidenceAgainst:
  - >-
    California's authority to set standards requires an active EPA waiver, which
    federal administrations can revoke or deny
  - >-
    The Trump administration revoked California's waiver in 2019, though this
    was later restored by the Biden administration in 2022
  - >-
    California's ability to write standards exists regardless of whether EPA
    abandons federal climate policy, as the authority predates current policy
    debates and has existed since the 1970 Clean Air Act
  - >-
    The claim conflates EPA weakening federal standards with California gaining
    new authority, when California's authority is a pre-existing statutory
    provision

whatThisMeans:
  - >-
    California possesses statutory authority under the Clean Air Act to set its
    own vehicle emissions standards, but this authority has existed for over 50
    years and is not newly granted by recent EPA policy changes
  - >-
    The claim suggests California gains authority because EPA is weakening
    federal standards, but California's authority exists independently through
    the waiver process and does not require EPA to abandon climate policy
  - >-
    California's practical ability to maintain stricter standards depends on
    retaining its EPA waiver, which has been subject to political changes
    between administrations
  - >-
    If EPA weakens federal standards while California maintains its waiver, a
    bifurcated regulatory system could emerge with California and states
    following its standards having stricter requirements than other states
