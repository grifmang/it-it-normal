# The FDA was changing leucovorin's label because it could help 'hundreds of thousands' of children with autism

## Claim Details

In 2025, statements were made that the FDA was changing the label for leucovorin because it could help hundreds of thousands of children with autism. This followed an announcement by FDA Commissioner Marty Makary in September 2025 regarding a label update for the drug.

## Evidence

### Label Change Announcement

FDA Commissioner Marty Makary announced in September 2025 that the FDA would update the label for leucovorin to reflect its use in children with autism and cerebral folate deficiency. This represented a shift in the agency's position on the drug's indication.

### Scope of Applicability

The FDA subsequently clarified that the label change applied specifically to children with cerebral folate deficiency, a rare metabolic condition that affects a small subset of children with autism spectrum disorder. Cerebral folate deficiency is characterized by low levels of folate in the cerebrospinal fluid despite normal folate levels in the blood.

### Discrepancy in Numbers

The claim that the label change would help "hundreds of thousands" of children appears to significantly overstate the scope. The FDA distanced itself from such characterizations, indicating the actual patient population was much smaller. Cerebral folate deficiency is a rare condition, not a widespread feature of autism spectrum disorder affecting hundreds of thousands of children.

### Context of Claims

The broader claims about leucovorin helping hundreds of thousands of children with autism appear to have originated from statements by HHS Secretary RFK Jr. and were subsequently disputed by the FDA when the agency provided clarification about the actual scope of the label change.

## Analysis

The claim contains elements of truth but presents a misleading picture. The FDA did initiate a label change for leucovorin related to autism treatment, confirming that part of the claim. However, the characterization that this change was because the drug could help "hundreds of thousands" of children with autism substantially overstates the scope of the change.

The label modification specifically addresses use in children with both autism and cerebral folate deficiency, a rare metabolic condition affecting a small patient population. The difference between this limited indication and the broader claim of helping hundreds of thousands of children represents a significant gap between the claim and the actual FDA action.
