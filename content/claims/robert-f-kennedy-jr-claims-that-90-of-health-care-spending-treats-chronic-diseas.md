# Robert F. Kennedy Jr. claims that 90% of health care spending treats chronic disease

## The Claim

Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has stated that 90% of healthcare spending treats chronic disease. This claim appears on RealFood.gov, a government website launched during his tenure as HHS Secretary nominee.

## What We Found

The claim relies on a CDC statistic, but involves a significant definitional issue. The CDC does state that 90% of the nation's $4.1 trillion in annual healthcare expenditures are for people with chronic and mental health conditions. However, this statistic measures total healthcare spending on people who have these conditions, not spending exclusively directed at treating those conditions.

This is a meaningful distinction. A person with diabetes, for example, may require treatment for an unrelated broken bone or routine preventive care. Under the CDC's methodology, all of that person's healthcare spending would be counted in the 90% figure, even though much of it is not treating the chronic condition itself.

Other research has produced lower estimates. The Milken Institute has estimated that treatment costs for chronic diseases comprise approximately 75% of healthcare spending, which attempts to isolate spending directly related to treating the conditions rather than all spending on people who have them.

The inclusion of mental health conditions in the CDC's 90% figure is another factor. The statistic combines chronic physical diseases with mental health conditions, which broadens the scope beyond what many might interpret as "chronic disease" in common usage.

## Context and Implications

Chronic diseases do represent the dominant driver of healthcare costs in the United States. Conditions such as heart disease, cancer, diabetes, and chronic respiratory diseases require ongoing medical care and account for substantial healthcare expenditures. The debate is not whether chronic diseases represent a major cost factor, but rather the precise percentage and how it is measured.

The distinction between spending "on people with" chronic conditions versus spending "treating" chronic conditions has policy implications. If the goal is to reduce healthcare spending through chronic disease prevention or management, understanding which costs are directly attributable to the conditions themselves affects projections of potential savings.

Federal health agencies including the CDC consistently identify chronic disease as a major public health and economic challenge, even if the exact percentage of spending varies by methodology.
